# Historical Context

Historic photographic techniques are experiencing an unprecedented revival in today's world. Combining modern digital technology with handcrafted 19th-century methods opens a path for creators to make distinct, tangible artistic originals without requiring a traditional darkroom.

---

## Digital Perfection vs. The Magic of Historic Prints

The development of photographic technology in recent years demonstrates that creating a technically flawless photograph has become quite simple even for beginners. Digital photography is capable of informing and documenting with great ease, and classical photography can hardly compete with it in that realm.

However, when examining the potential of digital photography as an individual artistic pursuit, we notice that uniform image editing software and the similarity of digital prints lead to a certain conformity of results. The photographs may be technically immaculate, but they all look strikingly similar.

We can expect that the easier digital camera technology and digital printing preparation become, the more people will want to turn back to classical photographic methods for their creative endeavors. Or combine digital and classical workflows – especially in creating the final appearance of the image in the process of transferring it onto paper to create a distinct, tactile original work of art.

Old photographs possess an irreplaceable magic. They are unrepeatable; every single image is crafted individually, and even when several prints are made from the exact same negative, no two are ever identical. On every photograph lies a significant share of personal creative work. Each image is unique – made with a different process, on different paper, with distinct tonality. Between carbon prints, platinum prints, and prints from a modern digital photo lab – however clean, grainless, and color-perfect the latter may be – there is a vast difference.

---

## The End of the Darkroom: The Digital Negative as a Bridge

In contemporary practice, historic photographic techniques based on **contact printing from a negative** are the most practical and widely used. Sensitizing paper for contact printing is far more feasible than preparing volatile wet-collodion glass plates.

Old photographic techniques print **by contact at a 1:1 scale** – the negative must be exactly the same size as the final photograph. In the past, creating such a negative with classical photography required either photographing with massive large-format cameras or laboriously producing duplicate large-format negatives in a darkroom, an undertaking that only experienced photographers with well-equipped labs could manage.

**Today, digital technology enables anyone to produce a large-format negative without a darkroom.** All that is required is capturing an image with an ordinary camera or smartphone, processing it with our web-based **Negative Creator**, and printing it onto a transparency film using a standard consumer printer.

Furthermore, historic contact printing methods do not require a traditional darkroom:
- The sensitized paper is prepared and handled under **ordinary weak incandescent tungsten light**.
- No optical enlarger is needed for exposure – an ordinary wooden contact printing frame with glass is sufficient.
- Exposure can take place outdoors in the sun, in a garden, or on a balcony.
- Developing and clearing require nothing more than clean water.

## The Invention of Cyanotype: Sir John Herschel (1842)

In the first half of the 19th century, European experimenters investigated numerous chemicals that could be used for "drawing with light." Following early experiments with silver salts (William Henry Fox Talbot) and mercury vapors (Louis Daguerre), the British astronomer, mathematician, and chemist **Sir John Herschel** turned his attention to the photochemistry of iron salts.

In 1842, Herschel presented his discovery of the cyanotype process to the Royal Society of London. He found that ferric salts of organic acids exposed to sunlight reduce to ferrous salts, which immediately react with potassium ferricyanide to form an insoluble, deep blue compound embedded directly in the fibers of paper.

::guide-note{title="Historical Figure"}
Sir John Herschel was a remarkably versatile scholar. In addition to cyanotype, he introduced the terms **"photography"**, **"negative"**, **"positive"**, and **"snapshot"** to the field, and discovered that sodium thiosulfate ("hypo") dissolved unexposed silver halides, providing photography with its universal chemical fixer.
::

## Anna Atkins & The World's First Photograph-Illustrated Book (1843)

Herschel's discovery was immediately embraced by his friend, the British botanist and illustrator **Anna Atkins**. Hand-drawing hundreds of delicate botanical specimens for scientific herbaria was arduous and imprecise.

Atkins began placing dried seaweeds and ferns directly onto sensitized cyanotype paper under glass and exposing them to sunlight (photograms). In October 1843, she published the first installment of her monumental work *Photographs of British Algae: Cyanotype Impressions*.

This work is historically recognized as **the very first book in the world illustrated with photographs** (preceding Talbot's *The Pencil of Nature* by several months). Her cyanotype prints have survived in museums and archives in pristine condition to this day.

## Technical Blueprints

From the 1870s onward, the Industrial Revolution created massive demand for rapid, inexpensive reproduction of technical and architectural drawings.

Draftsmen drew original plans with black ink on translucent tracing paper. The drawing was placed over large rolls of sensitized cyanotype paper and exposed to carbon arc lamps or sunlight. After a brief wash in water, engineers obtained an intensely blue print with crisp, bright white lines. Cyanotype thus gave rise to the international term **"blueprint"** and served engineering industries for more than a century.

## Overview of Historic Contact Printing Processes

According to their chemical principles, historic photographic processes are divided into three main groups:

### 1. Processes Based on the Light Sensitivity of Silver Salts
The sensitive compound is silver chloride, and the final image consists of metallic silver particles. Silver chloride can be dispersed in binders of various kinds:
- **Salt Print (Talbot 1834):** Suitable paper is coated with a solution of sodium chloride (table salt) and, after drying, brushed with silver nitrate solution, forming light-sensitive silver chloride. The image prints out directly during exposure (a printing-out process). After exposure, unreacted salts are washed out in water and fixed with sodium thiosulfate. The image consists of fine silver particles embedded directly in the paper fibers without a binder, yielding a reddish-brown color.
- **Albumen Print (1855–1895):** Thin paper is floated on egg white containing dissolved sodium chloride and, once dry, coated with silver nitrate. The silver image forms within an egg-white (albumen) binder layer on the paper surface, providing richer blacks, higher brilliance, and greater contrast. It accounted for the vast majority of surviving photographs from the second half of the 19th century.
- **Collodion and Gelatin Papers (from 1865 / 1884):** Factory-manufactured printing-out papers coated with a collodion or gelatin emulsion.

*Note on processing:* All of the above are **printing-out processes** (the image appears directly during exposure). They differ from **developing-out processes**, where exposure yields only a faint latent image that must be revealed through subsequent chemical developer baths.

### 2. Processes Based on the Light Sensitivity of Iron Salts
These use ferric salts of organic acids, which are reduced by the UV component of light to ferrous salts:
- **Cyanotype (1842):** The sensitizer contains ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferricyanide. Developed simply in water. The image prints out directly during exposure (a printing-out process) and is composed of insoluble Prussian blue.
- **Kallitype (1844):** Contains ferric oxalate and silver nitrate. A developing-out process (developed in borax or Rochelle salt solution). The image consists of pure metallic silver.
- **Platinum Print / Platinotype (1873):** Contains ferric oxalate and potassium chloroplatinite. The image is composed of pure metallic platinum particles embedded directly in the paper fibers, characterized by unrivaled permanence and supreme tonal nobility.
- **Van Dyke Brownprint (1889):** Contains ferric ammonium citrate and silver nitrate. A simple printing-out process yielding a warm reddish-brown silver image without a binder in the paper, fixed in hypo.

### 3. Dichromated Colloid Processes (Pigment & Non-Silver Prints)
Based on the fact that dichromated gelatin or gum arabic hardens when exposed to light, ceasing to swell in water and becoming insoluble:
- **Carbon / Pigment Print (Carbon Transfer):** Paper is coated with gelatin containing finely dispersed carbon or artist pigment. After exposure, the image is transferred to a support paper and unhardened gelatin is washed away in warm water.
- **Gum Bichromate (Gum Print):** Paper is coated with gum arabic, pigment, and potassium dichromate, developed in cold water. To achieve rich tonal gradations, it is frequently printed in multiple successive layers with repeated paper sizing.
- **Oil and Bromoil Prints:** The image is composed of greasy lithographic ink applied to a swollen, damp gelatin relief using specialized badger-hair brushes or foam rollers.

## Choosing a Process and the Advantages of Cyanotype

If we wish to try a historic process ourselves, it is best to begin with one that is simpler and financially accessible:

- **Accessibility & Simplicity:** Cyanotype is the least expensive, fastest, and technically least demanding process. We can achieve images just as striking as with much more costly techniques.
- **Pure Paper Aesthetic:** Because the image is embedded directly in the paper fibers without an emulsion binder layer, it feels much like an artistic lithograph and, unlike conventional photographs, does not curl under heat.
- **Subject Suitability:** Its intense blue color is ideally suited for snowy or misty landscapes, water surfaces, silhouettes, and botanical or technical subjects.
