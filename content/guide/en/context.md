# Historical Context

Cyanotype occupies a legendary place in the history of visual arts and science. Invented in 1842 during the birth of photography, it bypassed the expensive and finicky silver halides of its era in favor of iron photochemistry, birthing both the world's very first photographic book and the architectural "blueprint."

Today, cyanotype is experiencing an extraordinary renaissance as artists pair 19th-century tactile craftsmanship with precision modern digital negatives.

---

## The Invention: Sir John Herschel (1842)

In the early 1840s, scientists across Europe were racing to fix images "drawn by the rays of the sun." While William Henry Fox Talbot focused on light-sensitive silver salts (yielding the calotype) and Louis Daguerre perfected silvered copper plates (the daguerreotype), the English astronomer, mathematician, and chemist **Sir John Herschel** explored the light sensitivity of iron salts.

In 1842, Herschel presented his discovery of the cyanotype process to the Royal Society of London. He noted that ferric salts exposed to sunlight reduce to ferrous salts, which immediately react with potassium ferricyanide to deposit an insoluble, intensely blue dye directly within the cellulose fibers of paper:

## Anna Atkins: The First Photographic Book (1843)

Herschel's friend, British botanist and illustrator **Anna Atkins**, immediately recognized the immense scientific value of cyanotype. Hand-drawing thousands of delicate marine algae and seaweed specimens for botanical catalogs was tedious and imprecise.

In 1843, Atkins began placing dried algae specimens directly onto sensitized cyanotype paper, exposing them under glass in the English sunshine:

- In October 1843, she published the first installment of ***Photographs of British Algae: Cyanotype Impressions***.
- This monumental work is recognized worldwide by art historians as the **very first book in human history illustrated with photographic images**, preceding Fox Talbot's *The Pencil of Nature* by several months.
- Atkins produced hundreds of immaculate Prussian blue photograms that captured every microscopic frond and translucent membrane with scientific perfection.

## The Origin of the "Blueprint"

For the first four decades of its life, cyanotype was primarily used by botanists and hobbyists. However, by the 1870s and 1880s, the Industrial Revolution triggered an insatiable demand for rapid, low-cost duplication of large-scale engineering and architectural drawings.

Draftsmen drew master schematics in black ink on translucent tracing linen or vellum. This drawing was sandwiched against large rolls of cyanotype paper and exposed to carbon arc lamps. After a 3-minute wash in plain water, the exposed areas turned deep blue, while the black ink lines remained stark, pristine white.

This technology transformed global civil engineering, ship building, and city planning for over a century – giving our language the enduring idiom **"blueprint."**

## Alternative Photographic Processes in Comparison

In the 19th and early 20th centuries, photographers experimented with dozens of distinct chemical systems. As detailed in historical overviews, these processes generally divide into three major families:

| Process Family | Prominent Historic Techniques | Image Composition & Tone | Archival Permanence & Characteristics |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Silver Salt Processes** | **Salted Paper** (1834), **Albumen** (1855), **Collodion** (1865) | Warm reddish-brown to neutral dark gray; silver particles in egg-white or collodion binder | Prone to fading and atmospheric yellowing over decades; requires complex thiosulfate fixing. |
| **Iron Salt Processes** | **Cyanotype** (1842), **Kallitype** (1844), **Platinotype** (1873) | Saturated Prussian blue, rich sepia (Van Dyke), or neutral velvet black (Platinum) | **Exceptional archival permanence.** Pigment is locked directly in paper fibers without binder emulsion. Platinotype is noble metal; cyanotype does not crack, peel, or curl with heat. |
| **Dichromated Colloids** | **Carbon / Pigment Print** (1864), **Gum Bichromate** (1858), **Bromoil** (1907) | Any pigment color, painterly ink relief | Highly durable, tactile painterly surface; complex multi-stage transfers utilizing toxic chromates. |

## The Digital Negative: Modern Bridge to Historic Craft

Why are historical processes experiencing such an explosive renaissance today?

1. **The 1:1 Contact Requirement**: Historic alternative emulsions have very low ISO speeds. They cannot be enlarged with traditional photographic enlargers and must be contact printed in a **1:1 scale with the final paper size**.
2. **The 19th Century Bottleneck**: To make an A4 or A3 cyanotype print in 1860, a photographer had to carry a wooden view camera the size of a suitcase and fragile glass plate negatives into the field.
3. **The Digital Solution**: Modern digital cameras capture exquisite clarity. By using our digital converter, photographers can convert their photographs into negatives and print transparent film on desktop printers.

This convergence liberates artists from the darkroom, allowing anyone with a computer and a sunlit windowsill to create authentic, museum-grade 19th-century photographic prints.

## Remarkable Properties of Cyanotype

- **Self-Healing Prints**: Cyanotype is one of the few photographic processes capable of self-regeneration. If a cyanotype is hung in direct sunlight for too long, it may temporarily lighten as the pigment reduces to Fe²⁺. If placed in a dark drawer with fresh airflow, the print absorbs atmospheric oxygen and **spontaneously recovers its rich, dark blue depth**.
- **The Acid-Alkali Balance**: Cyanotypes thrive in slightly acidic conditions, but alkaline environments are their arch-nemesis. Washing a cyanotype with common household soap or displaying it behind alkaline-buffered mat boards will gradually decompose Prussian blue into yellowish iron hydroxide.
- **Cloth and Alternative Substrates**: Because the sensitizer is aqueous and requires no gelatin binder, it can be applied to cotton shirts, raw silk, linen, unfinished wood, plaster, and unglazed ceramic bisqueware to create permanent blue artwork.
