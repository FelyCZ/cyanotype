# Historic Photographic Processes – Overview

## The Allure of Old Photographs & The Renaissance of Historic Processes

The development of photographic technology in recent years demonstrates that creating a technically flawless photograph has become quite simple even for beginners. Digital photography is capable of informing and documenting with great ease, and classical photography can hardly compete with it in that realm.

However, when examining the potential of digital photography as an individual artistic pursuit, we notice that uniform image editing software and the similarity of digital prints—even for widely differing subjects—lead to a certain conformity of results. The photographs may be technically immaculate, but they all look strikingly similar.

We can expect that the easier digital camera technology and digital printing preparation become, the more people will want to turn back to classical photographic methods for their creative endeavors. Or combine digital and classical workflows—especially in creating the final appearance of the image, in crafting a distinctive artistic original, which essentially lies in the process of transferring the image onto paper.

One of the fundamental traits of classical photography based on the light sensitivity of silver salts is that many processes can be carried out by the photographer themselves, allowing them to embed their own creative vision into the work. The finished photograph can then take on many different forms. Every single image is a unique original. It is precisely this diversity of craft, the entirely individual character of the results, and the fact that we always obtain an original piece that will likely allow classical photography to endure—now, however, as a fine art technique.

Looking abroad, we find that so-called alternative photographic processes are enjoying ever-increasing popularity. These are essentially revived historic photographic techniques from the early days of photography. In the United States, an upsurge of interest has been evident since the 1980s, accelerating even further in recent years. Chemistry kits are commercially available there for nearly all historically used processes. Although some are quite expensive, enthusiasts eagerly purchase them. Platinotype is particularly popular, despite a kit of processing chemicals costing around $130 (more than 2,500 CZK). Dozens of books have been published on the subject, regular workshops are hosted, and numerous articles and discussion groups thrive online.

Old photographs possess an irreplaceable magic. They are unrepeatable; each is crafted completely individually, and even when several prints are made from the same negative, no two are ever identical. On every photograph lies a substantial share of personal creative labor. Each image is distinct—executed with a different process, on different paper, with distinct tonality. Between carbon prints, platinum prints, and machine-printed photographs from a modern minilab—however clean, grainless, and color-accurate the latter may be—there is an immense difference.

From the history of photography, we know that so-called "noble prints" (alternative pigment and oil processes) persevered for a very long time. For instance, oil prints and bromoil prints were created by many Czech photographers well into the 1930s, even though silver bromide photographic papers with a gelatin emulsion—of the type used essentially unchanged to this day—had long been widely available. Why did people remain devoted to these labor-intensive and time-consuming processes for so long? It seems that their greater malleability and expressive freedom brought them greater joy and satisfaction with the results.

Those seeking individual artistic expression find in historic photographic processes even broader possibilities than in traditional darkroom photography—here they can choose not only the technique (salt print, cyanotype, platinotype, oil print, etc.), but also the specific implementation (sensitizing formulation, paper grade, processing methods, and toning).

## Application in Modern Conditions & The Digital Negative

In contemporary practice, historic photographic techniques based on **contact printing from a negative** are the most practical and widely used. Sensitizing paper for contact printing is far more manageable than preparing volatile glass plates for the wet-collodion process. This means that the first link in the negative–positive chain, the negative, needs to be resolved in a different way.

Historic photographic techniques all print **by contact at a 1:1 scale**—meaning we require a negative of the exact same size as the intended final photograph. In classical photography, producing such a negative meant either shooting directly on large-format sheet film or laboriously making a duplicate large-format negative in a darkroom. Such a task could only be handled by an experienced photographer with a well-equipped darkroom.

**Today, digital technology enables the creation of large-format negatives without a darkroom.** Many authors believe that this single advancement has driven the enormous surge of interest in historic processes in recent years.

Historic photographic techniques otherwise do not require a traditional darkroom:
- Sensitized paper is prepared and handled under **ordinary weak incandescent tungsten light**.
- No optical enlarger is needed for exposure—an ordinary wooden contact printing frame with glass is completely sufficient.
- Exposure can take place outdoors, such as in the sun or on a balcony.

Free from dependence on traditional darkroom infrastructure, this entire creative field opens up to enthusiasts from outside traditional photography: all it takes is printing a digital negative onto transparency film, coating a suitable paper with the chosen sensitizing solution, contact-printing it, and then processing it under dim artificial light.

We can choose from many different processes, naturally taking into account our budget. If we build a pinhole camera and work with the salt print process, we can produce images at a lower cost than with conventional silver photography. If we opt for noble platinotype, however, we will pay roughly ten times as much.

What is certain is that the images are captivating and beautiful regardless of which technique is chosen—even with the least expensive, cyanotype. The substantial proportion of handcraft undoubtedly plays a major role, imparting an unmistakable air of fine artistry.

In the Czech Republic, historic photographic processes are perhaps still waiting to be widely discovered. Chemistry kits required for their processing are not yet commonly available on the local market, and ordering them from the US is relatively costly for the average enthusiast. There has been little literature in Czech on this subject. Foreign books are difficult to obtain and expensive. While some information is available on the Internet, only a minority read English fluently, and fewer still venture into their own experiments—having to source chemicals with difficulty and learn everything from scratch purely from literature. Complex processes are far easier to learn when someone demonstrates the workflow and points out potential pitfalls in person. The strongest inspiration for our own experiments comes when we see beautiful physical prints at an exhibition or in a friend's studio—something that is still rare here.

## Types of Historic Contact Printing Processes

According to their chemical principles and photosensitive layers, historic processes are divided into three main groups:

### 1. Processes Based on the Light Sensitivity of Silver Salts

The photosensitive substance is silver chloride, and the final image consists of metallic silver particles. Silver chloride can be dispersed in various types of binders:

#### Salt Print
A suitable paper is coated with a solution of sodium chloride (table salt) and, once dry, brushed with a solution of silver nitrate. This produces light-sensitive silver chloride. The image forms directly during exposure (a printing-out process). After exposure, residual silver nitrate and silver chloride are washed out in water, and the print is fixed with sodium thiosulfate. The image consists of fine silver particles embedded without any binder directly within the fibers of the paper. Because the silver particles are numerous and finely dispersed, the image exhibits a warm reddish-brown hue.

The salt print was first introduced by William Henry Fox Talbot in 1834. Salted papers were most widely used between 1840 and 1850 (concurrently with daguerreotypes). The vast majority of paper photographs from this decade were made using the salt print process. Some were toned with gold or platinum salts, yielding diverse hues ranging from brown-black and blue-black to neutral slate grey. Images toned with gold or platinum are highly resistant to atmospheric degradation and display remarkable longevity.

Due to its simplicity, ease of handling, and educational clarity, the salt print is exceptionally well suited for practice under modern conditions. While not entirely as trouble-free as the Van Dyke brownprint (discussed below), there is something almost magical in knowing that one is working in exactly the same way as photographers of the 1850s.

#### Albumen Process
Thin paper is floated on egg white (albumen) containing dissolved sodium chloride. After drying, it is sensitized with a silver nitrate solution. As with the salt print, the image is formed by fine particles of metallic silver, but rather than sitting directly in the raw paper fibers, they reside within the albumen binder layer on the paper surface. This enables deeper blacks, greater brilliance, and higher contrast.

The albumen process was the dominant photographic medium from 1855 to 1895—most surviving 19th-century photographs are albumen prints. The image is reddish-brown, much like the salt print, and was frequently toned with gold or platinum to increase permanence against environmental factors. However, albumen photographs almost invariably exhibit a yellowish cast in the highlights due to the natural aging of the egg-white binder.

#### Collodion Papers
Silver chloride is suspended in a binder of collodion and camphor. Collodion is cellulose tetranitrate dissolved in an ether-alcohol solution. The emulsion layer containing silver chloride is coated onto a paper support primed with a barium sulfate (baryta) and gelatin layer. The image consists of fine metallic silver particles within the binder and, like other printing-out processes, has a reddish-brown tone. The surface finish can be either glossy or matte.

Collodion papers were immensely popular and saw widespread use from 1865 until the 1920s. Most commercial studio portraits from that era were produced on collodion papers, frequently toned with gold or platinum salts.

#### Gelatin Papers
In appearance, gelatin printing-out papers (P.O.P.) are almost indistinguishable from collodion papers. However, gelatin is used as the binder instead of collodion. They were used from 1884 until the 1920s alongside collodion papers, though they initially faced stiff competition due to their higher price. The image again exhibited a reddish-brown tone and was routinely toned with gold or platinum.

::guide-note{title="Printing-Out vs. Developing-Out Processes"}
All of the silver processes described above are **printing-out processes** (*P.O.P.*). This means the image appears fully formed directly during exposure to light; subsequent chemical processing serves only to tone, clear, and stabilize it. This distinguishes them from **developing-out processes** (*D.O.P.*), where exposure produces only a faint or invisible latent image that must be brought forth through subsequent chemical developer baths.
::

### 2. Processes Based on the Light Sensitivity of Iron Salts

These processes utilize ferric (iron III) salts of organic acids, which are reduced by light (specifically the ultraviolet spectrum) to ferrous (iron II) salts. For example, ferric oxalate is reduced to ferrous oxalate. The resulting ferrous oxalate acts as a reducing agent that reduces ions of noble metals such as silver, platinum, or palladium into a solid metallic state, thereby forming the photographic image. The sensitizing solution is applied directly to the paper, embedding the final image straight into the paper fibers without an emulsion binder.

In cyanotype, instead of a noble metal salt, potassium ferricyanide is used to react with the photochemically produced ferrous salt, creating an insoluble Prussian blue pigment.

#### Cyanotype
The sensitizing solution consists of ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferricyanide. It is developed simply in water. The image appears directly during exposure, making it a printing-out process. It has been used since 1842.

Cyanotype is a relatively simple, fast, and economical process. Many people venturing into historic photographic techniques today begin with cyanotype as their first medium. If toned, the prints can take on a wide range of appearances. Toning can be done with tea, coffee, or tannic acid following a mild alkaline bleaching step. Lead or copper salts are also used historically. Depending on the toner, shades of grey, black, brown, red, or yellow can be achieved.

#### Kallitype
The sensitizer contains ferric oxalate and silver nitrate. The resulting image is composed of metallic silver. Exposure produces only a very faint image, which is intensified by subsequent development in a solution of borax or potassium sodium tartrate (Rochelle salt). It is therefore a developing-out process. It was introduced into practice in 1844.

#### Platinotype (Platinum Print)
The sensitizing solution contains ferric oxalate and potassium chloroplatinite. A faint image is formed upon exposure, which is then developed in a solution of potassium oxalate or another organic acid salt. The image consists of metallic platinum particles embedded directly within the paper substrate (introduced in 1873).

Platinotype is an exceptionally popular process in the United States, despite being relatively costly. Tradition undoubtedly plays a role here—many renowned 20th-century photographers created masterful portfolios with this technique, and encountering them in galleries naturally fosters the desire to make platinum prints of one's own. Another compelling factor is that this medium produces uniquely noble and valuable artworks capable of surviving in pristine condition not only through our lifetimes, but for generations to come.

#### Van Dyke Brownprint (Sepia Print)
The sensitizer consists of ferric ammonium citrate and silver nitrate. The image prints out directly during exposure. This is followed by a water wash and fixing in a dilute sodium thiosulfate bath. The image consists of metallic silver with a warm reddish-brown tone embedded directly in the paper without a binder. It has been in use since 1889.

The Van Dyke brownprint is another relatively straightforward and rapid process that presents fewer obstacles than many others. With suitable paper, one can achieve gratifying results on the very first try. The processing is equally simple—requiring no chemicals beyond standard fixer.

The entire process is quite affordable. The sensitizing solution requires only three common compounds. While silver nitrate is the most costly ingredient, only small quantities of solution are needed to coat each sheet, keeping running costs low. Furthermore, the finished print looks every bit as noble as far more intricate and expensive platinum prints. Should one desire platinum-level qualities, the finished Van Dyke prints can be toned with platinum or palladium salts (or both in combination). This produces an image composed of platinum and palladium—yielding identical tonal quality at approximately one quarter the cost of direct platinotype. By toning only the most successful prints, costs can be managed even more effectively.

The primary virtue of platinotype is its permanence. However, when exploring historic processes primarily for the joy of creation, archival permanence may not be the decisive factor. While silver-based printing-out images are somewhat less stable than modern baryta silver gelatin papers, they will nonetheless outlive us under reasonable storage. Surviving salt prints from the 1850s, though faded, remain remarkably legible and retain their unique historic charm.

### 3. Processes Based on the Light Sensitivity of Dichromated Colloids (Noble Prints)

These processes rely on the principle that gelatin or gum arabic (so-called colloids) sensitized with dichromates hardens upon exposure to light, ceasing to swell in water and becoming insoluble. The unexposed portions remain water-soluble and are washed away during development in water. In pigment prints (carbon transfer), the image is formed by pigment that was dispersed throughout the binder during preparation. In oil and bromoil prints, the image is formed by greasy printing ink applied subsequently onto the swollen gelatin relief created after washing away unhardened areas. In this way, crisp, rich continuous-tone images are achieved.

Before exposure, the colloid layer is sensitized with potassium or ammonium dichromate. It is exposed to light rich in UV radiation, developed with water, and requires no chemical fixer.

#### Pigment Print (Carbon Print)
A suitable paper is coated with a layer of gelatin containing finely dispersed pigment. This may be mineral pigment powder or fine lampblack—in which case it is called a carbon print.

Prior to exposure, the tissue is sensitized by immersion in potassium dichromate solution. After drying, it is contact-printed under a negative. The exposed tissue is soaked and squeegeed onto a transfer support paper (coated with hardened gelatin). The image adheres to the transfer paper such that its original bottom surface is now exposed on top. This allows warm water to dissolve and wash away all unexposed, unhardened gelatin and pigment from the back. The resulting image exhibits maximum density where the most light struck the tissue, hardening the gelatin—thus yielding a positive from the negative. The original temporary backing paper is discarded.

Pigment printing, especially the carbon print, was once beloved among Czech art photographers and is frequently encountered in historical exhibitions. Once the preparation of pigment tissues is mastered, it proves to be a relatively straightforward and efficient process. Because inorganic pigments are chemically inert, carbon prints boast permanence rivaling that of platinotype.

#### Gum Bichromate (Gum Print)
A solution of gum arabic and potassium dichromate, mixed with watercolor, gouache, or dry powder pigment, is brushed onto paper. Once dry, it is contact-printed in a printing frame under daylight. The image after exposure is only faintly visible. It must be developed in cold water, which dissolves the unexposed areas while leaving the exposed parts (the shadows) intact. Because the layer is thin, the exposed areas adhere firmly to the paper surface, eliminating the need for a transfer step as in carbon printing. Unexposed gum and pigment float away in the water bath down to the paper base. The print requires no fixer, as residual dichromate salts wash out concurrently.

Gum bichromate is structurally simple, yet producing fine results demands practice. One must also accept that a single-coat gum print cannot reproduce the full tonal scale of a negative; either shadow detail or highlight separation will be sacrificed depending on coating thickness, exposure time, and washing.

To achieve rich shadows alongside delicately articulated highlights, multiple successive printings are layered over one another. After the first exposure, development, and drying (which establishes midtones), the paper is recoated with a solution containing less gum and pigment, exposed longer, and developed until highlights emerge. Once dry, a third coat containing the highest concentration of gum and pigment is applied, exposed for the shortest time, and washed until only the deepest shadows remain.

Repeated coats are often necessary for optimal tonal nuance. Sizing the paper between successive printings is typically essential, as previous sizing washes out in water; without resizing, subsequent sensitizer sinks into the raw paper fibers, creating irreversible color staining in the purest highlights.

A multi-layered gum print with subtle tonal gradation is labor-intensive and cannot be finished in a single day. Nevertheless, single-layer gum prints can be delightful if one embraces their graphic character, selects fitting motifs, and prints predominantly for midtone aesthetics. Fortunately, gum printing is not the only option—negatives requiring smoother continuous tones can be rendered more easily via carbon transfer.

#### Oil Print
The oil print is based on the principle that a gelatin layer sensitized with dichromate hardens where struck by light and refuses to swell when subsequently soaked in water.

After contact exposure under a negative, the print is soaked in water: unexposed areas absorb water and swell, while exposed areas remain unswollen and firm. This swelling produces a physical relief where the raised areas correspond to highlights and depressions to shadows. When stiff, oily lithographic ink is dabbed onto this damp gelatin relief, it adheres exclusively to the exposed (unswollen, depressed) areas—and in direct proportion to how much light they received (how hardened they became). A continuous-tone positive image is thus created from the negative.

The ink is applied using specialized brushes, allowing the printmaker to deposit more or less ink, thicker or thinner, across different areas of the frame. The density and mood of various parts of the picture can therefore be modulated by hand. Unlike carbon or gum printing where pigment is present before exposure, oil printing applies ink post-exposure in controllable quantities. Furthermore, unsatisfactory inking can simply be cleaned off with petroleum spirits and reworked.

#### Bromoil Print
Bromoil differs from oil printing in that an ordinary silver bromide photographic enlargement is used as the starting point rather than contact exposure under a large-format negative. The photographic paper used must not possess an excessively hardened gelatin supercoat, or it will fail to swell adequately in water. Many resin-coated (RC) papers and unhardened traditional fiber-based (baryta) papers are suitable.

A silver print with well-articulated highlights and shadows is chemically bleached and tanned in a bath containing copper sulfate and potassium dichromate. It is then washed, fixed, washed again, and dried. The dried print is soaked in water to swell the gelatin relief and inked with lithographic inks just as in the oil process. In addition to traditional dabbing with badger-hair brushes, modern practitioners sometimes apply ink with high-density foam rollers.

The inked image can optionally be transferred onto another sheet of plain fine-art paper using a press. The matrix can then be re-inked to produce a limited edition of prints.

The visual results of bromoil and oil printing are equivalent, but bromoil offers distinct advantages: no large-format negative is required since one works directly from an optical enlargement; daylight contact printing is eliminated, along with the uncertainty of estimating solar exposure times; and selecting paper contrast grades provides easy control over final image contrast. However, the ease of preparing the bleached matrix does not diminish the dexterity and patience required for manual inking. Achieving mastery requires considerable practice and refined hand technique.

Due to these advantages, bromoil prints were immensely popular among Czech pictorialists through the 1920s and feature prominently in museum collections today.

## Choosing a Process

When setting out to explore historic photographic processes, it is advisable to begin with one that is technically straightforward and financially accessible. Fortunately, simpler and less costly techniques can yield images every bit as captivating as more elaborate and expensive ones. Every process possesses its own distinct personality and aesthetic qualities; success lies in understanding its nature and selecting subjects that harmonize with its character.

Even the least expensive technique—cyanotype—can produce extraordinary photographs. Its intense Prussian blue, however, is best suited to particular subjects, such as snow-covered or misty landscapes, water surfaces, silhouettes, and botanical or technical studies. Such scenes are ill-suited to the salt print, whose warm brown tones can make snow or fog appear unnaturally muddy. The salt print, conversely, excels at autumnal scenes with fallen foliage and sunlit architecture.

It is wise to choose processes that can be mastered with modest means and minimal initial experience, and pair them with appropriate imagery. Foremost among these are **cyanotype**, the **salt print**, and the **Van Dyke brownprint**.

These processes create an image embedded directly within the fibers of the paper without an emulsion binder. Free of a surface layer, the print feels much like an artist's stone lithograph. Unlike modern resin-coated photographs, it does not warp or curl with ambient temperature changes. By selecting art papers of complementary texture, weight, and tone, one creates a genuinely unique and unrepeatable original work of art.
